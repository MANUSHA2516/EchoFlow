CHAPTER 01

INTRODUCTION

This chapter introduces the research undertaken in this thesis. It begins by presenting the background to
patient queue management in public healthcare, with particular attention to the Echocardiography (ECO)
unit of Sri Lankan public hospitals, before narrowing to the specific problems that motivate this study. It
then states the research aim, objectives, and questions that guide the remainder of the thesis, defines
the scope of the proposed system with direct reference to the three interfaces implemented in this study,
discusses the significance of the research at patient, operational, managerial, national, and academic
levels, and closes by outlining the structure of the chapters that follow.

1.1 Background of the Study

Efficient patient flow is one of the most consistent challenges facing public healthcare delivery worldwide,
and it becomes especially critical in diagnostic and specialised units where equipment and specialist time
are limited resources. Queueing theory has long been applied to model and improve service systems in
healthcare, and a substantial body of research has established that structured queue management
directly influences patient satisfaction, staff workload, and the overall efficiency of a hospital unit [18]. As
patient volumes continue to grow in publicly funded hospitals, particularly in South Asian healthcare
systems, the absence of digital tools to manage this flow has increasingly been identified as a barrier to
service quality rather than a purely administrative inconvenience.

Sri Lanka's public healthcare system is widely regarded as one of the most accessible in the South Asian
region, offering free treatment at the point of care through a network of national, provincial, district, and
base hospitals. However, this accessibility has also produced very high patient volumes at government
hospitals, and outpatient and diagnostic units frequently operate with manual, paper-based registration
and queue-handling procedures. Several Sri Lankan studies have documented long and unpredictable
waiting times in outpatient departments (OPDs) of public hospitals, including at the National Hospital of
Sri Lanka, and have proposed operational strategies to reduce these delays [1]. Related prototypes have
explored patient registration and mobile alert systems for OPDs in Sri Lankan hospitals to reduce
overcrowding and improve communication with patients [2], while other local studies have investigated
automated patient-flow modelling and computer-vision-based approaches to understanding congestion
in OPD waiting areas [3], [4], [5]. Collectively, this body of work confirms that queue-related inefficiency
in Sri Lankan public hospitals is a well-recognised and actively researched problem, but also shows that
most existing local solutions address general OPD registration and communication rather than the specific
operational demands of a diagnostic unit such as Echocardiography (ECO).

The Echocardiography (ECO) unit occupies a distinctive position within cardiology services because it
depends simultaneously on limited numbers of trained sonographers or doctors, a restricted number of
ECO machines, and examinations that vary considerably in duration depending on the clinical indication.
International literature on echocardiography workflow confirms that scheduling and throughput
management in echo laboratories is a recognised operational challenge, driven by growing demand for
echocardiographic imaging relative to the availability of equipment and qualified staff [28], [29]. Recent
international research has gone further, applying machine-learning-enhanced queueing network
simulation specifically to hospital ultrasound operations, demonstrating that hybrid analytical and data-
driven models can more accurately represent and improve patient flow in imaging units than traditional
first-come-first-served scheduling alone [21]. This convergence of queueing science and machine learning
has become a defined research direction internationally, yet it remains largely unexplored in the context
of Sri Lankan public-hospital ECO units, where registration, queue numbering, and prioritisation are still
performed manually in most institutions.

At the same time, digital health has become a national policy priority in Sri Lanka. The Ministry of Health's
Digital Health Blueprint sets out a roadmap for coordinated digital health investment across the public
sector [8], and the National Digital Health Guidelines and Standards establish the technical and
governance expectations that any new clinical-adjacent digital system is expected to meet [9]. Public-
sector information systems such as the Hospital Health Information Management System (HHIMS) have
already been deployed to digitise parts of hospital administration [10], and private-sector doctor-
channelling platforms such as e-Channelling and Doc990 have demonstrated that Sri Lankan patients are
receptive to digital appointment and queue-related services when they are made available [6], [7].
However, these national platforms are primarily oriented towards appointment booking and general
hospital information management; none of them provide unit-level, real-time queue prediction or
priority-based queue management tailored to a high-demand diagnostic service such as ECO.

In parallel, the global body of research applying machine learning (ML) to healthcare wait-time prediction
has expanded rapidly. Studies using Random Forest, XGBoost, Gradient Boosting, and K-Nearest
Neighbour models have shown that patient waiting time in outpatient and diagnostic settings can be
predicted with meaningful accuracy from historical visit data, patient demographics, and temporal
features [11], [12], [13], [17]. More recent work has extended this to interpretable and dissatisfaction-
aware prediction models that account for the patient's perception of waiting [14], and to dynamic, self-
learning queue-management systems that combine predictive analytics with real-time queue state [15],
[16]. Reinforcement-learning-based approaches have also been explored for prioritising emergency and
urgent cases within a shared queue, rather than relying purely on arrival order [22], [23], [24]. These
developments provide the technical foundation on which the proposed system in this study is built,
adapting proven ML techniques such as Random Forest [26] and XGBoost [27] to the specific operational
data available in a Sri Lankan public-hospital ECO unit.

The feasibility of a patient-facing digital solution is further supported by broader trends in mobile
technology adoption. Sri Lanka has one of the higher rates of mobile broadband and smartphone
penetration in South Asia, and this has already enabled the growth of mobile-based healthcare services
such as doctor-channelling applications and IVR-based OPD reservation lines [6], [7], [25]. This existing
familiarity with mobile healthcare interactions among Sri Lankan patients reduces the adoption barrier
for a dedicated queue-tracking mobile application of the kind proposed in this study, and mirrors similar
mobile-augmented queue-management approaches documented in other developing-country hospital
settings [25].

It is against this background — a well-documented local queueing problem, a global trend towards ML-
based queue prediction, and a national digital health agenda that has not yet reached unit-level diagnostic
queue management — that this study proposes the design and development of an intelligent system to
manage patient flow and predict queue behaviour in the ECO section of a Sri Lankan public hospital. The
system is designed around three coordinated interfaces — a patient-facing mobile application, a staff
web portal, and an administrator web portal — the final implemented screens of which are presented
and described in detail in Chapter 04 (Results) of this thesis.
1.2 Problem Statement

Despite the growing demand for echocardiographic examinations in Sri Lankan public hospitals, the ECO
unit continues to rely on largely manual and paper-based processes for patient registration, queue
handling, and reporting. Preliminary investigation conducted as part of this research, together with the
wider literature on Sri Lankan OPD and hospital queue management [1], [2], [3], identified the following
interrelated problems:

    •   Manual patient registration: Patient details are recorded on paper, which is time-consuming to
        search and update, prone to loss or damage, and vulnerable to duplicate registrations.

    •   Long and unpredictable queues: A high daily volume of patients referred from multiple hospital
        departments creates long queues with no reliable way to estimate waiting time, which is
        consistent with findings reported in Sri Lankan OPD waiting-time studies [1] and in international
        queueing-in-healthcare literature [18].

    •   No formal queue-numbering system: Patients are generally served in the order they are
        remembered rather than through an enforced digital ticketing mechanism, leading to disputes
        over queue position and repeated staff interruptions.

    •   Absence of appointment scheduling: Patients arrive simultaneously rather than at scheduled
        intervals, producing avoidable overcrowding during peak hours.

    •   Difficulty prioritising urgent cases: Emergency or clinically urgent patients cannot be reliably
        identified and moved ahead of the queue under a manual system, a gap that recent priority-
        queue and reinforcement-learning research has specifically sought to address in other healthcare
        contexts [22], [23], [24].

    •   Limited doctors and ECO machines: A small number of sonographers/doctors and machines
        create an unavoidable service bottleneck, echoing the equipment- and staff-constrained nature
        of echocardiography workflow reported internationally [28], [29].

    •   Poor communication of waiting status: Patients are not informed of expected waiting times or
        delays, resulting in repeated queries to staff and reduced patient satisfaction.

    •   Lack of real-time monitoring: Hospital management has no live visibility of queue length, station
        status, or daily patient volume, making it difficult to detect and respond to congestion as it
        happens.

    •   Inaccurate resource and staff planning: Without historical trend analysis, staffing and resource-
        allocation decisions are made reactively rather than being informed by predictable demand
        patterns, despite strong evidence from international demand-forecasting research that patient
        volumes can be predicted from historical data with useful accuracy [30].

    •   Paper-based reporting and absence of historical analysis: Monthly and annual performance
        reports are compiled manually, which is time-consuming, error-prone, and does not support
        trend or pattern analysis for future planning.

These problems are not unique to Sri Lanka; however, the specific combination of a paper-based public-
hospital environment, a specialised and resource-constrained diagnostic unit, and the absence of any
locally deployed predictive queue-management tool represents a distinct and, to date, insufficiently
addressed research gap. Existing Sri Lankan prototypes have targeted general OPD registration and SMS-
based alerts [2], and existing national platforms such as e-Channelling and HHIMS operate at the level of
appointment booking and hospital-wide information management rather than real-time, ML-driven
queue prediction within a single diagnostic unit [6], [9]. Likewise, while international research has begun
to combine machine learning with queueing models for imaging services [21], this has not yet been
examined within a low-resource, public-hospital ECO setting such as that found in Sri Lanka. This study is
therefore motivated by the need for an integrated system that can digitally manage patient registration,
automatically generate and track queue numbers, prioritise urgent cases, predict waiting times and daily
patient demand using machine learning, and provide real-time analytical dashboards for hospital staff
and management — specifically for the ECO unit of a Sri Lankan public hospital.

The practical cost of leaving this problem unaddressed extends beyond patient inconvenience.
Unpredictable queues in a diagnostic unit such as ECO can lead to missed or delayed examinations,
extended patient stays elsewhere in the hospital while patients wait for an ECO slot, avoidable overtime
or congestion for clinical staff, and management decisions on staffing and equipment procurement that
are based on impression rather than evidence, consistent with the reactive resource-planning weaknesses
reported in the wider queueing-in-healthcare literature [18], [30]. Addressing these problems through an
integrated, ML-supported digital system therefore has the potential to produce measurable operational
improvements — reduced waiting time, improved patient satisfaction, more efficient staff utilisation, and
evidence-based resource planning — in a setting where the existing literature confirms these gains have
already been observed following digital or predictive queue interventions elsewhere [11], [15], [22].

1.3 Research Aim and Objectives

1.3.1 Research Aim

The aim of this study is to design, develop, and evaluate an intelligent, machine-learning-supported
system that digitally manages patient registration and queueing, prioritises urgent cases, predicts patient
waiting time and queue congestion, and provides real-time operational visibility for staff and
administrators in the Echocardiography (ECO) unit of a Sri Lankan public hospital.

1.3.2 Research Objectives

    1. To critically review existing local and international literature on hospital queue management,
       queueing theory, and machine-learning-based wait-time and demand prediction, in order to
       identify the specific research gap relevant to Sri Lankan public-hospital ECO units [1]–[24].

    2. To design and implement a digital patient registration and automated queue-ticket generation
       module, corresponding to the patient-facing Home Dashboard, Join Queue, and Live Ticket
       screens implemented in this study.

    3. To design and implement a priority-based queue management module that allows staff to
       identify, flag, and advance urgent cases ahead of routine patients, corresponding to the priority-
       tagging functionality on the Staff Queue Management screen, informed by priority-queue and
       reinforcement-learning approaches reported in the literature [22], [23], [24].
    4. To develop a machine-learning model capable of predicting patient waiting time and hourly/daily
       queue volume for the ECO unit using historical and real-time queue data, drawing on established
       algorithms such as Random Forest and XGBoost [26], [27] and informed by comparable healthcare
       wait-time prediction studies [11]–[17], corresponding to the Queue Prediction screen on the staff
       portal.

    5. To implement real-time queue tracking and status communication for patients, so that they can
       view their live position, estimated waiting time, and stage of their visit, corresponding to the Live
       Queue Tracking screen.

    6. To design and implement administrator-level oversight tools, including station/room
       management, staff-account management, an audit log, system settings, and analytical reporting
       dashboards, corresponding to the Administrator Web Portal screens implemented in this study.

    7. To evaluate the functionality, usability, and predictive accuracy of the developed system, and to
       document the results and their implications for public-hospital ECO queue management in Sri
       Lanka.

1.4 Research Questions

Based on the problem statement and research objectives above, this study seeks to answer the following
research questions:

    8. What are the primary causes of long and unpredictable patient waiting times in the ECO unit of
       a Sri Lankan public hospital, and how do they compare with causes documented in local and
       international literature?

    9. To what extent can a digital registration and automated ticketing system reduce manual
       administrative overhead and queue-position disputes in a public-hospital ECO unit?

    10. How effectively can a priority-based queue-management mechanism identify and advance
        clinically urgent patients without significantly disadvantaging routine patients in the same queue?

    11. How accurately can machine-learning models predict patient waiting time and hourly/daily queue
        volume for the ECO unit using available historical and real-time data?

    12. What real-time information and analytical tools do hospital staff and administrators require to
        effectively monitor and manage ECO-unit queue congestion, staffing, and resource allocation?

    13. How do patients and hospital staff perceive the usability and usefulness of the proposed mobile,
        staff, and administrator interfaces in a real or simulated ECO-unit setting?

1.5 Scope of the Study

This study is scoped to the design, development, and evaluation of a queue-prediction and patient-flow-
management system for a single diagnostic unit — Echocardiography (ECO) — within the context of a Sri
Lankan general/public hospital. The system is implemented as three coordinated interfaces, each
supporting a distinct user role, and the scope of this study is defined precisely by the final implemented
screens of those interfaces, presented in full in Chapter 04 (Results) of this thesis.
The Patient (User) Mobile Application is in scope and provides five core screens: a Home Dashboard
summarising the patient's current ticket and quick actions; a Join Queue screen for service/department
selection; a Live Ticket screen confirming the assigned queue number and status; a Live Queue Tracking
screen showing a staged, colour-coded timeline of the visit; and a Visit History screen listing past visits
with status badges.

The Staff Web Portal is in scope and provides seven core screens: a Today's Overview dashboard with key-
performance-indicator cards and a patient-volume chart; a Queue Management screen for calling,
serving, skipping, and prioritising patients; a Queue Prediction screen presenting machine-learning-
generated forecasts of patient influx, average wait time, and peak hours; a Patient Search screen; a Patient
Detail screen with visit history and a messaging panel; a Reports screen with exportable operational
statistics; and a Register New Patient modal for adding walk-in patients directly into the queue.

The Administrator Web Portal is in scope and provides eight core screens: a Today's Unit Overview
dashboard combining unit-wide key-performance-indicator cards, station status, and system alerts; a
Room/Station Management grid; a Staff Accounts management table; an Add-Staff-Member modal; an
Audit Log for recording system and user actions; a Settings screen for role-based permissions and security
configuration; a Register Station modal; and an Add Echo Room modal.

The machine-learning component of the system is limited to predicting patient waiting time and queue
volume/congestion for the ECO unit based on historical and real-time operational data (such as arrival
patterns, time of day, day of week, and station capacity); it does not extend to clinical diagnosis, image
analysis, or interpretation of echocardiographic results, which remain outside the scope of this study.
Similarly, the system is scoped to queue and patient-flow management within the ECO unit specifically,
and does not attempt to replace the hospital's existing Hospital Health Information Management System
(HHIMS) [10] or national e-Channelling appointment infrastructure [6]; rather, it is intended to
complement these systems at the unit level, in a gap that they do not currently address. Billing, insurance
processing, and full electronic health-record functionality are likewise excluded from the scope of this
study. The evaluation of the system is limited to functional testing, usability assessment, and predictive-
accuracy testing using available and/or simulated ECO-unit queue data, rather than a full multi-hospital
clinical trial.

1.6 Significance of the Study

This study is significant at the patient, operational, managerial, and academic levels.

For patients, the proposed system replaces uncertainty with visibility: rather than waiting without
information, patients using the mobile application can view their live ticket number, an estimated waiting
time, and a staged tracking timeline of their visit, directly addressing the poor communication of waiting
status identified in the problem statement and in Sri Lankan OPD literature [1], [2].

For front-desk and clinical staff, the Staff Web Portal is significant because it consolidates registration,
live queue management, priority handling, patient search, and reporting into a single digital workflow,
reducing the administrative burden associated with manual, paper-based processes and supporting the
kind of priority handling for urgent cases that manual systems in Sri Lankan public hospitals currently
struggle to guarantee [22], [23], [24].
For hospital administrators and management, the Administrator Web Portal provides, for the first time at
unit level, real-time oversight of station status, staff accounts, system alerts, and audit activity, together
with reporting and insight tools that support data-driven resource and staffing decisions rather than the
reactive planning described in the problem statement. This directly responds to the international evidence
that historical and real-time data can meaningfully inform hospital resource and demand planning [30].

At a national level, this study contributes to Sri Lanka's digital health agenda by extending digital-health
thinking from hospital-wide information management and appointment booking — the current focus of
platforms such as HHIMS and e-Channelling [6], [9], [10] — down to real-time, predictive, unit-level queue
management, an area explicitly identified as under-addressed in the existing literature and national digital
health guidance [8].

Academically, this study contributes to the growing international literature that combines machine
learning with queueing and patient-flow management in healthcare [11]–[17], [21]–[24], [26], [27], by
applying and evaluating these techniques within a previously unexamined context: a resource-
constrained, diagnostic (echocardiography) unit within a Sri Lankan public hospital. In doing so, it provides
an empirical case study that can inform both future Sri Lankan digital-health research and broader
research on ML-based queue management in low-resource healthcare settings internationally.

More broadly, the significance of this study extends to other resource-constrained diagnostic units
beyond ECO — such as radiology, laboratory, and endoscopy services — that share the same underlying
combination of limited equipment, limited specialist time, and manual queue handling. Because the
system architecture proposed in this study (digital registration, priority queueing, ML-based prediction,
real-time tracking, and administrative oversight) is not clinically specific to echocardiography, the findings
of this research are intended to offer a transferable model that other diagnostic units within Sri Lankan
public hospitals, and similarly resourced public-health systems in other developing countries, could adapt
for their own queue-management needs.

1.8 Chapter Summary

This chapter has established the foundation for the remainder of the thesis. It has shown that patient
queueing in the ECO unit of Sri Lankan public hospitals is a well-documented but still largely unaddressed
problem, situated between an active body of local OPD-focused research [1]–[5], a maturing international
literature on machine-learning-based queue and wait-time prediction [11]–[17], [21]–[24], [26], [27], and
a national digital health agenda that has not yet reached real-time, unit-level queue management [6],
[8]–[10]. From this background, the chapter derived a detailed problem statement, a clear research aim
and seven supporting objectives, six research questions, a scope defined precisely by the twenty
implemented screens across the Patient Mobile Application, Staff Web Portal, and Administrator Web

CHAPTER 02

LITERATURE REVIEW

2.1 Introduction

This chapter presents a critical review of the literature relevant to the design and development of an
intelligent patient-flow and queue-management system for Echocardiography (ECO) units in Sri Lankan
public hospitals. The review draws on thirty sources spanning four broad bodies of knowledge: (i) local,
Sri Lanka-specific studies and national digital-health policy documents that describe the current state of
outpatient department (OPD) operations and digitisation efforts; (ii) international literature on machine
learning (ML) approaches to predicting patient wait times and queue behaviour in clinical settings; (iii)
foundational queueing-theory and simulation research that provides the mathematical and
methodological basis for modelling patient flow; and (iv) domain-specific literature on echocardiography
workflow optimisation, priority queueing, Internet-of-Things (IoT)/mobile queue systems, and hospital
demand forecasting.

The chapter is organised as follows. Section 2.2 reviews the theoretical foundations of queueing theory
and simulation as applied to healthcare. Section 2.3 examines the Sri Lankan context, including national
digital-health policy and empirical OPD studies. Section 2.4 reviews global machine-learning approaches
to wait-time and queue-length prediction, together with the core algorithmic foundations (Random
Forest and XGBoost) that inform the predictive component of the proposed system. Section 2.5 discusses
priority queueing and AI-driven hospital queue-management systems. Section 2.6 reviews IoT and mobile-
based smart-queue architectures. Section 2.7 focuses specifically on echocardiography workflow
optimisation. Section 2.8 reviews hospital resource-demand forecasting literature. Section 2.9 provides
a critical, cross-cutting synthesis of the reviewed literature, and Section 2.10 consolidates the research
gaps that motivate the present study, mapping each gap to the corresponding feature of the proposed
system. Section 2.11 summarises the chapter.

2.2 Theoretical Foundations: Queueing Theory and Simulation in Healthcare
Queueing theory provides the mathematical foundation on which almost all quantitative queue-
management research is built, and it is therefore the natural starting point for this review. Fomundam
and Herrmann [18] provide a widely cited survey of queueing-theory applications in healthcare, classifying
prior work into categories such as bed-capacity planning, personnel and staff scheduling, and outpatient
waiting-time analysis. Their survey establishes the standard vocabulary of arrival and service distributions
(e.g., M/M/1, M/M/c and M/G/1 systems) that later, more applied studies build upon, and it highlights
an early and persistent observation in the field: that hospital arrival and service processes rarely satisfy
the strict statistical assumptions of classical queueing models, which motivates the shift toward simulation
and, more recently, machine-learning-based approaches.

Building on these theoretical foundations, a study on queue management in a Malaysian public hospital
[19] demonstrates how classical single- and multi-server queueing models can be adapted to a resource-
constrained, developing-country public hospital setting that is structurally comparable to Sri Lanka. The
study shows that even relatively simple queueing interventions, when combined with basic digitisation of
patient registration, can meaningfully reduce perceived and actual waiting times without requiring
additional clinical staff or equipment. This finding is particularly relevant to the resource constraints
described for ECO units in Sri Lankan public hospitals, where the number of doctors and echocardiography
machines is fixed in the short term.

A more recent study applies queueing theory and simulation specifically to emergency-department
waiting times through an Accumulative Priority Queue with a Finite Horizon [20]. This model addresses a
key weakness of both pure first-come-first-served (FCFS) queues and static priority queues: patients with
lower priority can be indefinitely delayed if higher-priority patients continue to arrive. The finite-horizon
accumulative priority mechanism allows a patient's effective priority to increase the longer they wait,
guaranteeing an upper bound on delay while still allowing urgent cases to be seen sooner. This is directly
relevant to the ECO context, where urgent cardiac referrals must be prioritised over routine outpatient
echocardiograms without indefinitely starving the routine queue.

The study most methodologically similar to the present research is a high-fidelity, machine-learning-
enhanced queueing-network simulation of hospital ultrasound operations [21]. Ultrasound, like
echocardiography, is a multi-stage, equipment- and operator-constrained imaging service, and the study
combines discrete-event simulation of the queueing network with ML-based surrogate models of patient
arrival and service-time distributions. This hybrid simulation-plus-ML approach demonstrates that
predictive models can be embedded inside a queueing-network simulation to produce far more realistic
estimates of waiting time and throughput than either technique alone, and it directly informs the
architectural approach adopted in the present study, which likewise couples an ML-based wait-time
predictor with a queue-management engine rather than treating prediction and queue control as separate
concerns.

2.3 Digital Health Infrastructure and OPD Queue Management in Sri Lanka

At the national-policy level, the Sri Lanka Digital Health Blueprint [8], published by the Ministry of Health,
Nutrition and Indigenous Medicine, sets out the government's strategic vision for digitising health
services, including outpatient and diagnostic services, and identifies interoperability and data
standardisation as key enablers. This is reinforced at a technical level by the National Digital Health
Guidelines and Standards, version 2.0 [9], which specifies the interoperability, data-exchange and system-
integration requirements that any new digital health system deployed in a Sri Lankan public hospital,
including the proposed ECO queue-management system, would be expected to satisfy. A retrospective
review of Sri Lanka's Health Information Management System (HHIMS) covering the period 1998-2017
[10] documents the country's two-decade digitisation journey, and highlights recurring adoption barriers
such as inconsistent infrastructure across hospitals, limited staff digital literacy, and fragmented,
department-level rather than hospital-wide system deployment. These barriers are directly relevant to
the design and rollout strategy of the proposed system.

At the empirical level, several Sri Lankan studies have examined OPD waiting-time problems directly.
Dilrukshi et al. [1] propose a strategy to reduce waiting time at the OPD of the National Hospital of Sri
Lanka, using simulation-based bottleneck analysis of the registration and consultation process to
recommend targeted resource reallocation. While effective as a diagnostic tool, the study does not
propose an ongoing digital system; it produces a one-off set of recommendations rather than a
deployable, continuously operating queue-management platform. Akmal Jahan et al. [2] describe OPD-
PMMAS, a prototype patient-management and mobile-alert system developed for OPDs in Sri Lankan
hospitals, which digitises patient registration and issues SMS-based alerts to inform patients of their
queue status. OPD-PMMAS is conceptually close to the notification and mobile-app components of the
system proposed in this thesis, but it remains at prototype scale, is not ECO-specific, and does not
incorporate any predictive or machine-learning capability for estimating waiting time or forecasting
demand.

A separate strand of Sri Lankan research has modelled OPD patient flow using discrete-event simulation
techniques [3], quantifying how changes in staffing levels and service-station allocation affect aggregate
waiting time; however, as with [1], the output is a simulation model for offline analysis rather than a live
operational system. Two further Sri Lankan studies apply computer-vision techniques to OPD operations:
one evaluates human-detection algorithms for automatically counting patients in OPD waiting areas [4],
and a companion study applies nameboard detection and text recognition to automatically identify clinic
and department signage [5]. Both studies demonstrate that computer-vision techniques can automate
data capture that would otherwise require manual staff effort, which is relevant to the real-time queue-
length monitoring and way-finding functions envisaged for the proposed system, although neither study
integrates its output into an end-to-end queue-management or prediction pipeline.

Finally, two studies describe existing, deployed digital-health services in Sri Lanka that represent the
current state of practice against which the proposed system can be differentiated. The eChannelling
digital OPD reservation service [6], launched by eChannelling PLC in partnership with ICTA Sri Lanka,
allows patients to book OPD appointments at district hospitals online, but it functions purely as a
booking/scheduling channel and does not perform live queue tracking, priority management, or predictive
waiting-time estimation within the hospital on the day of the visit. Similarly, the Doc990 mobile service
documented by GSMA Mobile for Development [7] uses basic mobile channels (voice/SMS) to improve
patient access to health information and services in Sri Lanka, illustrating the importance of low-friction
mobile access in a context where not all patients own smartphones, but again without any queue-
prediction or in-hospital flow-management functionality. Together, these studies confirm that while
digital OPD services exist in Sri Lanka, none combines patient registration, real-time priority-aware queue
management, and ML-based wait-time prediction into a single system targeted at a specific diagnostic
unit such as echocardiography.

2.4 Machine Learning Approaches to Wait-Time and Queue-Length Prediction

A substantial and growing international literature applies supervised machine-learning techniques to
predict outpatient and clinic waiting times, and this literature forms the primary methodological basis for
the predictive component of the proposed system. Li et al. [11] predict outpatient waiting time in a tertiary
children's hospital using a range of ML models trained on features such as registration time, department,
and physician workload, and report that tree-based ensemble methods consistently outperform
traditional linear regression approaches. Their results validate the feasibility of ML-based wait-time
prediction in large, complex hospital settings with multiple departments and physicians, a structural
profile that is comparable to the multi-doctor, multi-machine environment of an ECO unit.

A related study published in Informatics in Medicine Unlocked [12] compares several machine-learning
algorithms for predicting wait times in an outpatient clinic and emphasises the importance of feature
engineering, particularly features that capture a patient's real-time position in the queue and the time of
day, over simply adding more algorithmic complexity. A 2024 preprint [13] extends this line of work to
predicting patient wait times together with appointment delays, which is relevant because unpredictable
delays and no-shows directly affect the accuracy of any real-time queue-length estimate; a robust ECO
queue-prediction model must therefore account for both service-time variability and non-attendance.

An important methodological contribution comes from a 2024 study that frames wait-time prediction as
a dissatisfaction-considered, interpretable machine-learning problem [14]. Rather than optimising only
for predictive accuracy, the study explicitly incorporates patient-reported dissatisfaction as an outcome
and stresses model interpretability (for example, through feature-importance or SHAP-based
explanations) so that clinical and administrative stakeholders can trust and act on the model's predictions.
This directly informs the design choice, adopted in the present study, to favour interpretable tree-
ensemble models over opaque deep-learning architectures for the ECO wait-time predictor.

Guo et al. [15] propose machine-learning-based prediction models embedded within a dynamic hospital
queue-management system, rather than as a standalone offline prediction tool, which is conceptually the
closest precedent to the integrated prediction-plus-management architecture proposed in this thesis. A
separate JMIR Medical Informatics study conducted in a paediatric hospital [16] validates ML-based wait-
time prediction on a large-scale, real-world retrospective dataset, providing evidence that such models
can generalise beyond small pilot studies to practical, real-world deployment. Finally, a 2025 comparative
study published in the International Journal of Engineering Trends and Technology [17] benchmarks
multiple machine-learning algorithms against one another for predicting consultation wait times in
outpatient clinics, providing a direct evidence base for algorithm selection.

The consistent finding across [11], [15], [16] and [17] is that ensemble tree-based methods, and gradient-
boosted trees in particular, tend to outperform both simple regression baselines and, in several cases,
more complex neural architectures on structured, tabular hospital operations data of the kind that an
ECO unit would generate (patient demographics, referral source, time of arrival, doctor/machine
allocation, and historical service durations). This motivates a closer examination of the two core
algorithms that underlie most of these studies and that are adopted as the primary candidate algorithms
for the predictive module of the proposed system.

2.4.1 Core Machine Learning Algorithms: Random Forest and XGBoost

Breiman's Random Forest algorithm [26] is a bagging-based ensemble method that constructs a large
number of de-correlated decision trees over bootstrap-resampled subsets of the training data and
features, and aggregates their predictions by averaging (for regression) or majority voting (for
classification). Random Forests are well suited to noisy, mixed-type healthcare operational data because
they are relatively insensitive to outliers and missing values, require minimal feature scaling or
preprocessing, and provide a natural measure of feature importance that supports the interpretability
requirement identified by [14]. These properties make Random Forest a strong baseline candidate for
predicting ECO queue length and waiting time from heterogeneous inputs such as patient category,
referral urgency, time of day, and current queue occupancy.

Chen and Guestrin's XGBoost [27] is a scalable, regularised implementation of gradient-boosted decision
trees that builds an ensemble of trees sequentially, with each new tree trained to correct the residual
errors of the preceding ensemble. XGBoost incorporates second-order gradient information, L1/L2
regularisation, and efficient handling of sparse data, and has become one of the dominant algorithms for
structured/tabular prediction tasks across many domains, including several of the wait-time prediction
studies reviewed above (notably [11] and [17], both of which report gradient-boosted trees among their
top-performing models). Given its consistent superiority on structured healthcare datasets in the
literature reviewed, and its practical advantages in training speed and predictive accuracy over plain
Random Forests when properly tuned, XGBoost is adopted as the primary candidate algorithm for the
ECO queue-length and wait-time prediction module in the proposed system, with Random Forest retained
as an interpretable benchmark model.

2.5 Priority Queueing and AI-Driven Hospital Queue Management

Beyond prediction alone, several recent studies combine artificial intelligence with priority-queueing logic
to actively manage, rather than merely forecast, hospital queues. A 2024 study optimises hospital queue
management using a priority-queue algorithm combined with reinforcement learning for emergency-
service prioritisation [22], showing that a reinforcement-learning agent can learn dynamic prioritisation
policies that adapt to real-time queue conditions, outperforming static priority-level rules of the kind
used in most existing systems, including the finite-horizon accumulative priority model of [20]. This
suggests that a future extension of the proposed ECO system could move from static priority weighting
(e.g., urgent versus routine referral) toward a learned, adaptive prioritisation policy.

MediQueue [23], a 2025 ML-driven hospital queue-management system, is architecturally close to the
system proposed in this thesis: it combines predictive modelling with active queue orchestration in a
single deployable system rather than treating prediction as a separate analytical exercise. As such,
MediQueue serves as a useful comparative benchmark for the system-design decisions and evaluation
metrics (e.g., prediction accuracy, average wait-time reduction, queue-length error) adopted later in this
thesis. Similarly, Kavitha and Maragathasundari [24] propose AI-enabled queueing-theory applications
for hospital patient-flow optimisation, integrating classical queueing performance metrics (e.g.,
utilisation, average queue length, average waiting time) with AI-based optimisation techniques. Their
work reinforces a recurring theme across this section of the literature: that combining classical queueing
theory with modern AI/ML techniques consistently outperforms either approach used in isolation, which
is the design philosophy adopted for the ECO queue-management engine proposed in this study.

2.6 IoT and Mobile-Based Smart Queue Systems

The patient-facing component of the proposed system, comprising a mobile application through which
patients join a queue, receive a live ticket number, and track their position in real time, draws on the
broader literature on IoT and mobile-based smart-queue systems. An early but influential study describes
a smart mobile system for the real-time tracking and management of service queues [25]. Although
developed for general service industries rather than healthcare specifically, the system's core
architectural pattern, comprising remote ticket issuance, push-notification-based status updates, and live
queue-position tracking on a patient's own device, is directly transferable to the healthcare context and
closely mirrors the Home Dashboard, Join Queue, Live Ticket, and Live Queue Tracking screens
implemented for the patient mobile application in the present study. The absence of a healthcare-specific,
ML-integrated equivalent of this architecture in the literature reviewed represents a further gap that the
proposed system addresses.

2.7 Workflow Optimisation in Echocardiography Services

Literature focused specifically on echocardiography workflow, as distinct from general OPD or emergency-
department queue management, is comparatively scarce, which itself constitutes an important
motivation for this study. A review published in US Cardiology Review [28] identifies the principal
bottlenecks that constrain throughput in echocardiography laboratories, including scheduling
inefficiencies, sonographer and physician availability, equipment turnaround time between studies, and
reporting/sign-off delays. The review is largely descriptive and does not propose or evaluate a specific
digital or predictive intervention.

Solis et al. [29] present a more applied study on optimising echocardiography workflow to increase patient
access to high-quality care, proposing process-level changes such as protocol standardisation,
appointment triage, and more efficient scheduling of studies by complexity, in order to increase
throughput without requiring additional machines or staff. This is the most clinically and operationally
relevant precedent for the ECO-specific focus of the present research. However, neither [28] nor [29]
incorporates machine-learning-based prediction of waiting time or queue length, nor a real-time digital
queue-management system with a patient-facing mobile interface; both rely on manual process redesign
rather than an automated, data-driven system. This absence of a predictive, technology-enabled solution
specifically for echocardiography services represents one of the clearest gaps addressed by this thesis.

2.8 Demand Forecasting for Hospital Resource Planning
Finally, a 2025 study published in the Gulf Journal of Advance Business Research examines the forecasting
of hospital resource demand using a combination of time-series and machine-learning models [30].
Although the study addresses hospital-wide resource demand (such as bed occupancy and staffing needs)
rather than a single diagnostic unit, its methodological approach, combining classical time-series
techniques with machine-learning models to forecast future demand, is directly transferable to
forecasting daily and weekly patient volumes for an ECO unit. This informs the design of the Queue Length
Forecasting feature and the administrator-facing analytics dashboard proposed in this thesis, extending
general hospital demand-forecasting principles to the specific, previously unaddressed context of
echocardiography services in a Sri Lankan public hospital.

2.9 Critical Analysis and Synthesis

Read together, the thirty sources reviewed in this chapter reveal four consistent patterns. First, the Sri
Lanka-specific literature ([1]-[7]) demonstrates clear awareness of OPD queue-management problems
and has produced valuable prototypes and analytical studies, but almost none of these systems
incorporate predictive machine learning, and none is specific to echocardiography or any single
diagnostic-imaging unit; most also remain at prototype or one-off analytical scale rather than being
deployed as production systems, a gap partially explained by the infrastructure and adoption barriers
documented in the national HHIMS review [10].

Second, the international machine-learning literature ([11]-[17], [26], [27]) provides strong, repeated
empirical evidence that tree-based ensemble methods, particularly gradient-boosted trees, can predict
outpatient wait times with useful accuracy, and that interpretability matters for clinical adoption [14].
However, this literature is overwhelmingly generic to outpatient clinics in general or to specific non-
cardiac specialties (e.g., paediatrics [11], [16]), and none of the reviewed studies addresses
echocardiography or any comparably equipment-constrained, multi-stage imaging service.

Third, the queueing-theory, simulation, and priority-queueing literature ([18]-[24]) provides strong
theoretical and methodological tools, including finite-horizon accumulative priority disciplines [20] and
reinforcement-learning-based dynamic prioritisation [22], for managing urgent and routine cases fairly
and efficiently. The ML-enhanced ultrasound simulation study [21] and the MediQueue system [23]
demonstrate that combining these theoretical tools with ML prediction in a single deployable system is
both feasible and effective, but, again, no reviewed study applies this combined approach to
echocardiography specifically, nor within the Sri Lankan public-hospital context.

Fourth, the domain-specific echocardiography literature ([28], [29]) confirms that ECO-specific workflow
bottlenecks are real and clinically significant, but the proposed remedies are limited to manual process
redesign rather than automated, predictive, technology-enabled solutions. The IoT/mobile smart-queue
literature [25] and hospital demand-forecasting literature [30] each provide transferable architectural
and analytical components (real-time mobile tracking; time-series/ML demand forecasting) that have not
previously been assembled together, nor applied to an ECO unit.

2.11 Chapter Summary

This chapter has reviewed thirty sources spanning Sri Lanka-specific OPD and digital-health studies, global
machine-learning approaches to wait-time and queue-length prediction, foundational queueing-theory
and simulation research, priority-queueing and AI-driven hospital queue-management systems,
IoT/mobile smart-queue architectures, echocardiography-specific workflow-optimisation studies, and
hospital resource-demand forecasting. The critical synthesis in Section 2.9 shows that, while each

queueing, real-time mobile tracking, and demand forecasting) has precedent somewhere in the literature,
no existing study or system integrates all of these components together, and none does so specifically for
echocardiography services within a Sri Lankan public-hospital context. Section 2.10 formalised this
observation into five specific, literature-grounded research gaps and mapped each to a corresponding
feature of the system proposed in this thesis. These gaps directly motivate the research aim, objectives,

CHAPTER 03

METHODOLOGY

3.1 Introduction

This chapter presents the research methodology adopted for the design, development, and evaluation of
an intelligent patient-flow and queue-management system for the Echocardiography (ECO) unit of a Sri
Lankan public hospital. As established in Chapter 2, the study addresses a set of literature-grounded
research gaps by combining digital patient registration, machine-learning-based wait-time and queue-
length prediction, priority-aware queue management, and real-time mobile/web interfaces into a single,
integrated system, delivered through the Patient Mobile Application, the Staff Web Portal, and the
Administrator Web Portal.

This chapter is submitted as the methodology component of Interim Submission 01 and therefore focuses
on the research design, the data-collection strategy, the development tools and technologies, and the
ethical considerations that govern the study, consistent with the chapter breakdown specified in the
project guidelines. The detailed presentation of the resulting system implementation (the finalised
patient, staff, and administrator interface screens) is reported separately in Chapter 4 (Results), and the
evaluation of those results is discussed in Chapter 5 (Discussion); this chapter therefore describes how
the system was planned, designed, and built, and how the supporting data and evaluation instruments
were collected, rather than the outcomes themselves.

The remainder of the chapter is organised as follows: Section 3.2 describes the overall research design;
Section 3.3 describes the system-development methodology adopted; Section 3.4 presents the functional
and non-functional requirements derived from the literature review and stakeholder needs; Section 3.5
describes the proposed system architecture; Section 3.6 describes the data-collection strategy; Section
3.7 lists the tools and technologies used; Section 3.8 describes the machine-learning model-development
process; Section 3.9 describes the testing strategy; Section 3.10 discusses the ethical considerations
addressed by the study; and Section 3.11 summarises the chapter.

3.2 Research Design

This study adopts a Design Science Research (DSR) approach, since its primary contribution is a
purposefully designed, evaluable software artefact, namely the ECO queue-management system, rather
than the testing of a pre-existing theoretical hypothesis alone. DSR is well suited to this study because it
explicitly requires the researcher to (i) identify a real-world problem, addressed in Chapter 1 and
reinforced by the research-gap analysis in Chapter 2; (ii) define objectives for a solution; (iii) design and
develop an artefact; (iv) demonstrate the artefact's use; (v) evaluate how well the artefact addresses the
problem; and (vi) communicate the results, which corresponds directly to the Chapter 1 to Chapter 5
structure of this thesis.

Within this overarching design, a mixed-methods approach is used. A quantitative strand is applied to the
development and evaluation of the machine-learning wait-time and queue-length prediction model,
using historical and simulated queue data and standard regression-evaluation metrics (Section 3.8). A
qualitative and quasi-experimental strand is applied to requirements elicitation and usability evaluation,
using structured interviews, observation of the existing manual ECO queue process, and prototype
walkthroughs with representative staff and patient participants. Combining these two strands allows the
resulting system to be both technically sound (an accurate, well-evaluated prediction model) and
operationally appropriate (an interface and workflow that genuinely fits how ECO staff and patients
work), which single-method designs relying on only one of these strands would not guarantee.

3.3 System Development Methodology

The system itself is developed using an Agile, iterative-prototyping methodology, rather than a single-
pass waterfall process, because the three user-facing applications (patient, staff, and administrator) each
require multiple rounds of stakeholder feedback before a workable design is reached, and because the
machine-learning component benefits from incremental refinement as more representative data
becomes available. Figure 3.1 illustrates the six-stage cycle followed for each development iteration:
requirements gathering, system and UI/UX design (wireframing), prototype development, iterative
implementation in short sprint builds, testing and evaluation, and refinement based on stakeholder
feedback, after which the cycle repeats for the next set of features or screens.

In practice, this cycle was applied first to establish the overall information architecture of the system (the
three-portal structure described in Section 3.5), then to progressively wireframe and prototype each
screen, beginning with the core patient journey (Home Dashboard, Join Queue, Live Ticket, and Live
Queue Tracking), followed by the staff-side operational screens (Today's Overview, Queue Management,
and Queue Prediction), and finally the administrator-side management screens (Today's Unit Overview,
Room/Station Management, Staff Accounts, Audit Log, and Settings). Low-fidelity wireframes were first
produced to validate screen flow and information hierarchy, before being refined into the high-fidelity
mockups presented in Chapter 4. This staged approach ensured that each interface was validated against
the functional requirements in Section 3.4 before implementation effort was committed to it.

3.5 System Architecture
The proposed system follows a layered, multi-client architecture, illustrated in Figure 3.2. At the
presentation layer, three separate client applications are provided for the three user roles identified in
the requirements analysis: the Patient Mobile Application, the Staff Web Portal, and the Administrator
Web Portal. All three clients communicate with a shared backend server through a common REST
Application Programming Interface (API), which centralises authentication, ticket issuance and state
management, notification dispatch, and core business logic, ensuring that an action performed on one
interface (for example, a staff member marking a ticket as complete on the Queue Management screen)
is immediately reflected on the corresponding patient-side interface (the Live Queue Tracking screen).

Below the API layer, the architecture separates two concerns: a Machine Learning Prediction Service,
responsible for generating queue-length and wait-time forecasts consumed by the Queue Prediction
screen and by the estimated-wait-time indicators shown in the patient application; and a Central
Database, which persists patient records, tickets, station and staff data, and the audit log. The ML
Prediction Service is trained offline using historical queue and appointment records (Section 3.6 and
Section 3.8) and is queried by the API layer at inference time, allowing the prediction model to be
retrained and redeployed independently of the rest of the system as more operational data becomes
available, without requiring changes to the client applications.

3.6 Data Collection

Two categories of data are collected to support this study: requirements/evaluation data, used to design
and validate the system's interfaces and workflow, and operational data, used to train and evaluate the
machine-learning prediction model.
Requirements and evaluation data are collected directly from prospective users of the system at a
selected Sri Lankan general hospital's ECO unit. Two participant groups are targeted: (i) clinical and
administrative staff directly involved in the current manual queue process, including receptionists/clerks,
nursing staff, and the medical officers responsible for performing echocardiograms; and (ii) patients
attending the ECO unit for routine or urgent examinations. Data-collection instruments include semi-
structured interviews with staff to elicit the functional requirements summarised in Table 3.1, direct, non-
participant observation of the existing manual registration and queueing process to document current
bottlenecks (corroborating the problems identified in Chapter 1), and structured prototype walkthroughs
with both staff and patients, in which participants are asked to complete representative tasks (for
example, joining a queue, or calling and completing a ticket) using the Figma prototypes before
implementation, and again using the implemented screens during usability testing (Section 3.9).

Operational data for the machine-learning component is drawn, subject to the ethical and access
approvals described in Section 3.10, from retrospective, de-identified ECO attendance and scheduling
records maintained by the hospital, covering attributes such as appointment/arrival date and time,
referral source and urgency category, assigned doctor and echo room, recorded service start and end
time, and patient demographic category (age band and sex, without any directly identifying information).

timeframe, a supplementary, literature-informed synthetic dataset is generated, using arrival- and
service-time distributions consistent with the queueing-theory and simulation literature reviewed in
Chapter 2 (notably [18], [20], and [21]) and the general hospital demand-forecasting approach of [30], to

3.8 Machine Learning Model Development Process

The wait-time and queue-length prediction model is developed following a standard supervised-learning
pipeline. Raw data collected as described in Section 3.6 is first cleaned and pre-processed: missing values
are imputed or the corresponding records excluded, categorical attributes (referral source, priority
category, assigned doctor/room) are encoded, and derived features such as day-of-week, hour-of-day,
and rolling historical average queue length for the corresponding time slot are engineered, consistent
with the feature-engineering emphasis identified in the literature review (notably [12] and [14]).

The resulting dataset is partitioned into training and test subsets (a standard 80/20 split, with k-fold cross-
validation applied on the training subset for hyperparameter tuning) so that reported performance
reflects the model's ability to generalise to unseen queue conditions rather than merely fitting historical
data. Consistent with the comparative evidence presented in Chapter 2 (Section 2.4.1), two candidate
algorithms are trained and compared: Random Forest [26], used as an interpretable benchmark model,
and XGBoost [27], used as the primary candidate model given its consistently strong performance on
structured, tabular healthcare-operations data reported in [11] and [17]. Model performance is evaluated
using standard regression metrics, namely Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE),
and the coefficient of determination (R²), which together capture both the average magnitude of
prediction error and the proportion of variance in actual wait times explained by the model.

The selected model is exposed as an internal prediction service (Section 3.5) that the API layer queries to
populate the estimated-wait-time indicators on the Patient Mobile Application and the hourly forecast
chart on the Staff Portal's Queue Prediction screen. Because the model is intended to be retrained
periodically as new operational data accumulates, feature-importance outputs from both the Random
Forest and XGBoost models are also retained and reviewed, in line with the interpretability requirement
identified in [14], so that hospital administrators can understand which factors most strongly influence
predicted wait times.

3.9 System Testing Strategy

The system is tested at three levels, consistent with the iterative development methodology described in
Section 3.3. Unit testing is applied to individual backend functions (for example, ticket-priority calculation
and API endpoint responses) to confirm that each component behaves correctly in isolation. Integration
testing verifies that the three client applications, the API layer, the ML prediction service, and the database
interact correctly, for example confirming that a ticket marked complete on the Staff Portal's Queue
Management screen is reflected within an acceptable latency on the Patient Application's Live Queue
Tracking screen.

Usability testing is conducted with representative staff and patient participants recruited through the
same process described in Section 3.6, using structured task scenarios (for example, joining a queue and
interpreting the live ticket screen as a patient; calling, prioritising, and completing tickets as a staff
member) and the System Usability Scale (SUS) questionnaire to obtain a standardised, comparable
usability score for each interface. Finally, user-acceptance testing (UAT) is conducted with hospital staff
to confirm that the finalised system, as presented in Chapter 4, satisfies the functional requirements in
Table 3.1 and is fit for the intended operational context before any recommendation for deployment is
made.

3.10 Ethical Considerations

Because this study involves human participants (hospital staff and patients) and, potentially, hospital
records containing personal information, it raises a number of ethical considerations that are addressed
as follows.

First, formal ethical approval is sought from the relevant university research-ethics review body prior to
any data collection, and, given that data collection takes place within a public hospital, administrative
permission is separately sought from the hospital's own ethics or administrative review board and from
the ECO unit's clinical lead. No interviews, observation, or prototype testing involving staff or patients is
conducted prior to these approvals being granted.

Second, participation in interviews, observation, and usability testing is entirely voluntary. All participants
are provided with a plain-language information sheet describing the purpose of the study, what
participation involves, and how their data will be used, and are asked to provide informed consent (or,
for observation of routine clinic operations, are informed of the observer's presence and purpose) before
taking part. Participants retain the right to withdraw at any point without any effect on their care or
employment.

Third, data privacy and protection are addressed throughout the study in line with Sri Lanka's Personal
Data Protection Act, No. 9 of 2022. Any hospital records used to train the machine-learning prediction
model are de-identified before use, with direct identifiers (patient name, national identity number, and
contact details) removed or replaced with non-reversible study identifiers, retaining only the operationally
relevant attributes listed in Table 3.2. Access to any identifiable data that is unavoidably required during
the requirements-gathering phase (for example, to link a specific interview participant's feedback to their
role) is restricted to the researcher and stored securely, separately from the operational dataset used for
model training.

Fourth, the system design itself incorporates features that support ethical and secure data governance in
eventual deployment, namely the Audit Log, which records a timestamped, user-attributed history of
system actions for accountability, and the Settings screen's role-and-permission matrix and security
controls (including two-factor authentication and session timeout), which restrict access to patient data
on a need-to-know basis according to staff role.

Finally, because the study concerns the prioritisation of patients, including urgent cardiac cases, care is
taken that the priority-queueing logic (Section 3.4, FR4) is designed and evaluated to avoid introducing or
amplifying unfair treatment of any patient group, and that the machine-learning prediction model is
evaluated for consistency of performance across patient categories, rather than optimising only for
average accuracy across the whole dataset.

3.11 Chapter Summary

This chapter has presented the research methodology adopted for this study, framed as a Design Science
Research project combining quantitative machine-learning model development with qualitative,
stakeholder-driven requirements analysis and usability evaluation. It described the Agile, iterative-
prototyping approach used to move from requirements through wireframes and Figma prototypes to the
implemented system presented in Chapter 4, the functional and non-functional requirements derived
from the research gaps identified in Chapter 2, and the layered system architecture connecting the three
client applications to a shared API, machine-learning prediction service, and central database. It further
described the data-collection strategy for both requirements/evaluation data and machine-learning
training data, the tools and technologies used, the machine-learning model-development pipeline built
around Random Forest and XGBoost, the three-level testing strategy, and the ethical considerations,
including institutional approvals, informed consent, data de-identification, and fairness in priority-queue
design, that govern the conduct of the study. Together, these elements provide the methodological basis
for the system implementation results presented in Chapter 4 and the evaluation and discussion
presented in Chapter 5.

5.1 Introduction
    This chapter discusses the system implementation results presented in Chapter 4, the twenty
finalised interface screens of the Patient Mobile Application, the Staff Web Portal, and the
Administrator Web Portal, in relation to the research problem set out in Chapter 1, the literature
and research gaps identified in Chapter 2, and the methodology described in Chapter 3. Where
Chapter 4 reported what was built, this chapter interprets why it matters: it evaluates how far
the implemented system addresses the fourteen operational problems originally identified in
the ECO unit, examines the extent to which each of the five research gaps identified in Section
2.10 has been closed by the delivered design, compares the resulting system against the existing
Sri Lankan and international systems reviewed in Chapter 2, and reflects critically on the study's
implications, limitations, and directions for future work.

    Because this thesis is reported at the stage of a completed system design and interface
implementation rather than a live, multi-month clinical deployment, the discussion in this
chapter is necessarily framed around the design decisions embodied in the implemented screens
and the extent to which they satisfy the functional and non-functional requirements set out in
Table 3.1, rather than around long-run clinical outcome data (for example, measured reductions
in mean patient waiting time over a full operational period), which falls outside the scope of the
interim system delivered and is instead identified as a priority for the pilot evaluation
recommended in Section 5.10. Any figures shown within the implemented Reports, Queue
Prediction, or Dashboard screens in Chapter 4 (for example, the illustrative KPI values on the
Staff Dashboard) are sample data used to demonstrate the analytics interface and are not
presented here as measured clinical results.

    The remainder of the chapter is organised as follows. Section 5.2 discusses the implemented
system as a whole in relation to the original research problem. Sections 5.3 to 5.5 discuss the
Patient Mobile Application, Staff Web Portal, and Administrator Web Portal in turn. Section 5.6
evaluates the achievement of the research objectives and the closure of the five research gaps
identified in Chapter 2. Section 5.7 compares the resulting system with existing and related
systems. Section 5.8 discusses the theoretical, practical, and methodological implications of the
study. Section 5.9 discusses its limitations, and Section 5.10 sets out recommendations for future
work, before Section 5.11 summarises the chapter.


5.2 The Implemented System in Relation to the Research Problem
    Chapter 1 identified fourteen interrelated operational problems affecting the ECO unit,
ranging from manual, paper-based registration and the absence of any queue-numbering system,
through poor communication of waiting times and difficulty prioritising urgent cases, to the
absence of real-time monitoring, historical data analysis, and reliable demand forecasting. Read
against this list, the twenty implemented screens reported in Chapter 4 can be understood as a
coherent, role-differentiated response: rather than a single generic application, the system
deliberately separates the patient-facing concern (visibility and self-service), the staff-facing
concern (operational control of the live queue), and the administrator-facing concern (unit-wide
oversight, staffing, and governance), because the underlying problems identified in Chapter 1
are themselves distributed across these three roles and cannot be adequately addressed by a
single undifferentiated interface.

    This role-based separation directly mirrors the structure of the requirements analysis in
Section 3.4 (Table 3.1) and confirms that the requirements-elicitation and iterative-prototyping
methodology described in Chapter 3, moving from wireframes through Figma prototypes to the
finalised screens, produced an information architecture that traces cleanly back to the originally
identified problems rather than to an arbitrary or generic queue-management template. The
following three sections examine each of the three interfaces in turn.


5.3 Discussion: Patient Mobile Application
    The five patient-facing screens (Home Dashboard, Join Queue, Live Ticket, Live Queue
Tracking, and Visit History) directly target the communication and predictability problems
identified in Chapter 1, specifically the observations that patients are not informed about
expected waiting times, that delays are not communicated effectively, and that patients
repeatedly ask staff about their turn. By surfacing a persistent, estimated-wait-time badge on
the Home Dashboard and a colour-coded, stage-by-stage timeline on the Live Queue Tracking
screen, the implemented design converts what was previously tacit, staff-mediated information
("how much longer?") into an always-available, self-service data point on the patient's own
device. This is consistent with the design rationale established in the literature reviewed in
Chapter 2: the OPD-PMMAS prototype [2] demonstrated that SMS-based status alerts
measurably reduce patients' need to repeatedly query staff, and the general-purpose smart-
queue architecture of [25] shows that live, mobile position-tracking is both technically feasible
and well understood as a pattern; the patient application implemented here extends this pattern
from a generic service queue to the specific, higher-stakes context of a diagnostic-imaging unit.
    The Join Queue screen's department/service selector and queue-type toggle operationalise
the digital-registration objective identified in Chapter 1 (replacing paper-based registration) and
in the research-gap analysis (G1, Section 2.10), while directly addressing the duplicate-
registration and misplaced-record problems associated with manual registration. It is worth
noting, however, that the patient application assumes smartphone ownership and a reasonable
level of digital literacy; the GSMA case study on Doc990 [7] specifically highlights that not all
patients in the Sri Lankan public-health context have smartphone access, which is a limitation
discussed further in Section 5.9 and which motivates the SMS-fallback recommendation in
Section 5.10.


5.4 Discussion: Staff Web Portal
    The seven staff-facing screens address the operational core of the problems identified in
Chapter 1: the absence of a queue-numbering system, the difficulty of prioritising urgent cases
manually, staff shortages and administrative overload, and the lack of real-time monitoring. The
Queue Management screen's "Now serving" card with Call/Skip/Complete actions, together with
its colour-coded priority tag for urgent cases, operationalises the priority-queueing requirement
(FR4, Table 3.1) that was informed directly by the accumulative priority-queue model of [20] and
the reinforcement-learning-based dynamic prioritisation approach of [22] discussed in Chapter
2; while the implemented interface currently exposes a simpler, staff-assigned urgent/normal
priority tag rather than the fully adaptive reinforcement-learning policy described in [22], it
establishes the interaction surface (a visible, actionable priority indicator within the live-queue
list) on which such a policy could be layered in future iterations, as discussed in Section 5.10.

    The Queue Prediction screen is the clearest embodiment of the machine-learning
contribution motivated in Chapter 2: its hourly forecast chart and recommended-action panel
operationalise the wait-time and queue-length prediction pipeline described in Section 3.8 (built
around Random Forest [26] and XGBoost [27]), giving staff the kind of forward-looking, demand-
forecasting capability that Chapter 1 identified as entirely absent from the current manual
process and that the reviewed literature ([11], [15], [16], [17]) shows to be both feasible and,
when interpretable, trusted by clinical stakeholders [14]. The Patient Search, Patient Detail, and
Register New Patient screens jointly replace the paper-based registration and record-searching
problems identified in Chapter 1, while the Reports screen operationalises the shift from paper-
based, manually compiled monthly reporting toward on-demand, exportable operational
reporting. Collectively, these seven screens demonstrate that the staff portal is not simply a
digitised version of the paper register, but an integrated operational control surface that
combines registration, live-queue control, predictive analytics, and reporting in a single, role-
appropriate interface, consistent with the MediQueue architecture [23] discussed in Chapter 2
as the closest existing precedent for this kind of integrated design.


5.5 Discussion: Administrator Web Portal
    The eight administrator-facing screens address problems in Chapter 1 that are unit-wide
rather than per-patient in nature: inaccurate, reactive resource planning, the inability of hospital
management to monitor queue lengths across the unit, and the absence of any reliable
mechanism to estimate future workload for staffing purposes. The Today's Unit Overview
dashboard, with its station-status table and system-alerts panel, gives administrators the
"control-room" visibility that Chapter 1 identified as missing, while the Room/Station
Management and Add Echo Room screens operationalise resource planning at the level of
physical capacity, including the auto-scheduling toggle, which represents a direct design
response to the equipment-bottleneck problem highlighted in both Chapter 1 and the
echocardiography-specific workflow literature reviewed in Chapter 2 [28], [29].

    The Staff Accounts and Add Staff Member screens address the staff-shortage and
administrative-overload problems from a governance perspective, by making staff capacity and
role allocation directly visible and manageable rather than tracked informally. The Audit Log and
Settings screens were not derived from a problem explicitly listed in Chapter 1, but rather from
the ethical and data-governance considerations discussed in Section 3.10; their inclusion reflects
a design decision to treat accountability and access control as first-class system requirements
from the outset, rather than as an afterthought once the operational features were complete,
which is consistent with the requirement, established in Chapter 3, that any system handling
patient data in a Sri Lankan public hospital must be designed with the Personal Data Protection
Act, No. 9 of 2022, in mind from the earliest design stages.

5.8 Theoretical, Practical, and Methodological Implications
    Theoretically, this study contributes a concrete demonstration that the individually well-
established components identified in the Chapter 2 literature, digital registration, priority
queueing, ML-based wait-time prediction, and real-time mobile tracking, can be integrated into
a single, coherent system architecture for a specific, equipment-constrained diagnostic service,
extending the general hospital-queue-management literature ([15], [22], [23], [24]) into the
previously unaddressed echocardiography context and extending the echocardiography-specific
workflow literature ([28], [29]) from manual process redesign into an automated, data-driven
design.

    Practically, the implemented system offers Sri Lankan public-hospital ECO units a design that
could plausibly be adopted with modest infrastructure investment, since it builds on architectural
patterns (REST APIs, relational databases, cross-platform mobile frameworks) that are already in
use elsewhere in the national digital-health ecosystem described by the Digital Health Blueprint
[8] and the NDHGS v2.0 [9], rather than requiring an entirely bespoke technology stack.
Methodologically, the study demonstrates the practical value of combining a Design Science
Research framing with an Agile, iterative-prototyping process (Chapter 3) for health-information-
systems research in a resource-constrained public-hospital setting: the staged progression from
wireframes to Figma prototypes to implemented screens, validated at each stage against the
functional-requirements table (Table 3.1), provided a disciplined mechanism for keeping the
resulting system traceable to the original research problem, rather than drifting toward generic
queue-management functionality disconnected from the ECO-specific context.


5.9 Limitations of the Study
    Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, and most significantly, the results
reported in Chapter 4 represent a completed interface design and implementation rather than
a live, multi-month clinical deployment; consequently, this discussion is necessarily based on
design-level analysis and traceability to requirements rather than on measured operational
outcomes such as actual mean-wait-time reduction, real patient-satisfaction scores, or validated
model-accuracy figures obtained from live ECO data, all of which require the pilot deployment
recommended in Section 5.10.

    Second, the machine-learning prediction pipeline described in Section 3.8 was designed and,
where hospital-record access allows, trained using a combination of retrospective historical data
and literature-informed synthetic data (Section 3.6); until the model has been retrained and
validated on a sufficiently large volume of real, multi-month ECO attendance data from the target
hospital, its predictive accuracy in a live setting cannot be reported with confidence, and the
reported approach should be understood as establishing a validated methodology rather than a
field-tested predictive model.

    Third, the study is scoped to a single hospital's ECO unit; while the underlying architecture is
designed to be extensible (Section 3.5), the specific findings and design decisions discussed in
this chapter may not generalise without modification to ECO units with substantially different
patient volumes, staffing levels, or infrastructure, nor to other diagnostic-imaging services
without further requirements analysis specific to that service. Fourth, the patient-facing
application assumes smartphone access and a baseline level of digital literacy, which, as noted
in Section 5.3, is not universal among the Sri Lankan public-hospital patient population, meaning
that a meaningful subset of patients may not be able to use the mobile application without staff
assistance or an alternative access channel. Finally, formal, statistically powered usability
evaluation (System Usability Scale scoring and user-acceptance testing, as described in Section
3.9) had not been completed for the full set of twenty screens at the time of this interim
submission, and the usability-related claims made in Sections 5.3 to 5.5 should therefore be read
as design-rationale claims grounded in the literature and requirements traceability, rather than
as empirically validated usability findings.


5.10 Recommendations for Future Work
    Building on the limitations identified above, five directions for future work are
recommended. First, a time-bound pilot deployment of the system within a single ECO unit,
following the ethical-approval and data-governance procedures described in Section 3.10, should
be conducted to collect the real operational and usability data needed to validate the design-
level claims made in this chapter, including formal SUS-based usability scoring and user-
acceptance testing across all three interfaces. Second, the machine-learning prediction model
should be retrained and re-evaluated on real, multi-month ECO attendance data as it becomes
available through the pilot, replacing or supplementing the literature-informed synthetic training
data used during development (Section 3.6), and its accuracy should be benchmarked using the
metrics identified in Section 3.8 (MAE, RMSE, R²) against real held-out data.

    Third, the current staff-assigned priority tag in the Queue Management screen could be
extended toward the adaptive, reinforcement-learning-based prioritisation approach
demonstrated by [22], allowing the system to learn dynamic prioritisation policies from observed
queue behaviour rather than relying solely on a static urgent/normal classification, which would
more fully close research gap G2 identified in Table 5.1. Fourth, to address the smartphone-
access limitation discussed in Section 5.9, an SMS or basic-mobile fallback channel, informed by
the Doc990 model [7] and the SMS-alert component of OPD-PMMAS [2], is recommended as a
complementary access route for patients without smartphones, so that the benefits of live queue
visibility are not limited to smartphone owners. Fifth, once validated within the ECO unit, the
underlying architecture (Section 3.5) should be evaluated for extension to other resource-
constrained, equipment-bound diagnostic services within the same hospital, such as radiology
or ultrasound, building on the precedent set by the ML-enhanced ultrasound-simulation study
[21] and testing the generalisability limitation identified in Section 5.9 directly.


5.11 Chapter Summary
    This chapter has discussed the twenty implemented interface screens reported in Chapter
4 in relation to the research problem established in Chapter 1, the literature and research gaps
identified in Chapter 2, and the methodology described in Chapter 3. It examined the Patient
Mobile Application, Staff Web Portal, and Administrator Web Portal in turn, showing that each
addresses a distinct cluster of the operational problems originally identified, and it evaluated the
extent to which the implemented system closes the five research gaps identified in Section 2.10,
finding three gaps substantially addressed and two partially addressed pending further
validation. A comparison with existing and related systems showed that the proposed system is,
to the best of the evidence reviewed, the first to combine real-time in-hospital tracking, ML-
based prediction, priority handling, and diagnostic-imaging specificity in a single design. The
chapter also discussed the study's theoretical, practical, and methodological implications,
acknowledged its limitations, most importantly that the reported results reflect a completed
design and implementation rather than a validated live deployment, and set out five
recommendations for future work, centred on a pilot deployment, real-data model retraining,
adaptive prioritisation, a non-smartphone access channel, and extension to other diagnostic
services. These findings, together with those of Chapters 1 through 4, form the basis for the
overall conclusions of this thesis.




5.10 Recommendations for Future Work

Based on the limitations presented above, five possible directions of future work are proposed. Firstly, an
implementation trial with a defined time span of the system within one ECO unit in accordance with the
ethical approval and data governance protocol presented in Section 3.10 should be carried out to gather
the real-life data on operational performance and usability needed to validate the claims made in this
chapter at the design level, including the use of SUS scoring and user acceptance testing of all three
interfaces. Secondly, the machine-learning prediction model should be retrained and reevaluated based
on real, multi-month ECO attendance data provided through the implementation trial, either replacing or
complementing the synthetic literature-informed training data used during development (Section 3.6),
with the accuracy benchmarked in terms of MAE, RMSE and R² metrics, outlined in Section 3.8, against
real held-out data.

Thirdly, the current priority tag assigned by staff on the Queue Management screen could be expanded
towards the adaptive reinforcement learning-based prioritisation policy demonstrated in [22], thus
closing the research gap G2 identified in Table 5.1. Fourthly, in order to tackle the issue of limited
smartphone access highlighted in Section 5.9, an SMS and basic mobile access channel, inspired by Doc990
[7] and OPD-PMMAS [2] SMS alert module, is recommended as an alternative option for patients not
owning smartphones, making the benefits of the live queue visibility accessible beyond the users of
smartphones. Finally, after successful implementation within the ECO unit, the architecture of the system
(Section 3.5) should be assessed for further extension to other diagnostic services in the hospital,
constrained by similar resource and equipment limitations, namely radiology or ultrasound, with the
example of ML-enhanced ultrasound simulation [21] setting the precedent.
